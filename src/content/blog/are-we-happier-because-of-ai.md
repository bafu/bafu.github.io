## TL;DR

AI should make us happier, not more anxious.

But in mid-July 2026, I saw a different picture. Threads was full of AI-generated content and people selling AI courses. Open models had entered national-security and regulatory debates. Kimi K3 brought Chinese open models closer to the leading US closed models. Robot fighting became entertainment in Shenzhen. Elon Musk said work would eventually become optional.

These events look separate. But they point to the same question: as AI changes content, work, competition, and governance, are people becoming freer, calmer, and more able to decide who they want to be?

My answer is: **not yet.**

We have become more capable, but not necessarily happier. Capability is only the first step. If control, purpose, and the distribution of value do not keep up, AI may push us toward a different kind of anxiety — or toward a future where we are gently cared for but no longer need to think.

---

## The Fatigue Started on Threads

Recently, my Threads feed has filled with AI-generated content and posts selling AI services and courses.

At first, Threads reminded me of the PTT I remember, Taiwan's long-running online bulletin board [12]. Not because the interfaces or cultures were the same, but because of the variety. Some people wrote about technology. Others wrote about life, politics, bad jokes, or simply how they felt that day. That mix was valuable. It made the internet feel like it still had real people behind it.

Lately, that feeling has faded.

In its place is a familiar rhythm: use AI to publish one hundred posts a day, build your personal brand, make money automatically, or avoid being left behind. Every post seems to say the same thing: if you are not anxious yet, you do not understand how anxious you should be.

I find this exhausting.

Not because I oppose AI-generated content or AI education. I use AI every day. I am also building a sovereign AI system for my company. The problem is not AI itself. The problem is that when the cost of producing content approaches zero, and algorithms reward high-frequency output, the internet will naturally fill with whatever is cheapest to make, easiest to copy, and best at triggering emotion.

We used to spend time reading to understand another person's ideas.

Now we spend time scrolling, trying to decide whether the words in front of us reflect someone's thinking or are simply the by-product of a prompt.

This is not happiness. It is a new tax on the mind.

## The Week AI News Seemed to Speed Up

In mid-July, I saw a series of AI stories that looked unrelated but became striking when placed together.

The first was anxiety over the regulation of open models.

On July 12, Nathan Lambert wrote about the possibility that the US could take stronger policy action against highly capable open-weight models within the next few months. Joseph Jacks extended the discussion. He argued that if the White House tried to ban Mythos-tier open-weight models, such a ban would be difficult to enforce. It would also revive an old question about free speech and whether code is protected expression [1][2].

This concern did not appear from nowhere. On June 12, Anthropic said the US government had asked it to suspend access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for foreign users because the government believed their safeguards could be bypassed. To comply, Anthropic ultimately took the models offline for all customers. On June 30, the US government lifted the Fable 5 restriction. Axios described the period as an 18-day interruption [8][9].

For most users, this may have looked like one model becoming temporarily unavailable.

But for companies and products built on AI, it was another warning. AI is not only becoming technical infrastructure. It is becoming policy infrastructure. Whether a model remains usable no longer depends only on API stability, price, or capability. It also depends on regulators, model companies, national competition, and public opinion.

The second story was Kimi K3.

On July 17, Kimi K3 was the model I saw discussed most often. Moonshot announced Kimi K3 on July 16, describing it as an open 3T-class model with 2.8 trillion parameters, a one-million-token context window, and vision capabilities. The full weights were scheduled for release on July 27 [10].

I saw many social posts claim that Kimi K3 had surpassed Fable 5 and GPT-5.6. The more precise version is that Moonshot itself acknowledged Kimi K3 still trailed the strongest closed models overall. But in specific evaluations involving long-horizon coding, frontend work, tool use, and knowledge work, it had come very close and sometimes moved ahead [10].

The important part is not who won.

The important part is this: **open models have taken another major step forward, and they are moving much faster than policy can.**

If an open model is already close to the strongest closed models, does banning it reduce risk? Or does it simply reduce the number of people who can use, study, and verify it? If the US imposes restrictions while open-source communities in China and elsewhere keep moving forward, American companies and developers lose options. Does that actually make anyone safer?

The third story was robot fighting.

That same week, videos of robot combat from China filled my X feed. According to Anue, EngineAI held the opening night of the world's first URKL humanoid robot fighting league in Shenzhen on July 16. The event featured one-on-one fights, five rounds, tactical pauses, repairs, replacement robots, a round of 32, a final, and a large prize pool [3].

It would be easy to watch a clip, find it entertaining, and keep scrolling.

But placed in the same week as Kimi K3, it felt different. AI is no longer only answering questions in a text box or writing code inside an IDE. It is entering robot bodies, entertainment, industry showcases, and the way cities present their technological strength.

The future of AI used to feel abstract. Now it is concrete: model weights on one side, robot fists on the other.

## When Regulation Becomes a Competitive Strategy

On July 19, PANews reported that David Sacks had reposted and responded to comments by Dean W. Ball, OpenAI's Head of Strategy and Futures, about Chinese open AI models. According to the report, Sacks argued that if the government used poorly supported regulatory guidance to create fear, uncertainty, and doubt — pressuring regulated companies to stop using Chinese open models — it would weaken the rule of law and create a dangerous precedent for future regulatory abuse. He also said leading closed-model companies were trying to use government power to weaken open competition [4][5].

I do not want to reduce this to supporting or opposing Sacks.

Politicians, investors, model companies, and open-source communities all have their own positions and interests. What matters is that once AI becomes a national competition, regulation can easily become more than a public safety tool. It can also become a tool for commercial competition.

That is dangerous.

Once regulation becomes a competitive strategy, public debate starts to bend. Safety risks may be real, but they can also be amplified. The risks of open models may be real, but so are the risks of concentrating power in closed models. Chinese models may raise concerns about model safety, data governance, and national security. It is also reasonable to worry that US closed-model companies may use government power to build a moat.

If the discussion cannot return to facts, logic, and evidence, AI policy becomes a more sophisticated form of fear marketing.

It is the same thing I see on Threads, just at a different level.

At the individual level: "Learn AI or you will be replaced."

At the company level: "Use this tool or your competitors will leave you behind."

At the national level: "Ban this kind of model or the enemy will win."

Each statement may contain some truth. But if every level is driven by fear, people will not become happier. They will become more obedient.

## If Work Is Optional, Are We Happier?

Earlier this year, Elon Musk repeated an appealing vision of the future: within 10 to 20 years, AI and robots could make work optional and perhaps make money irrelevant [6].

It is a beautiful vision.

If people no longer had to work to survive, spend most of their time on repetitive labor, or suppress what they really wanted to do just to cover the cost of living, that would clearly be progress.

I have long believed AI will complete the last piece of the Industrial Revolution. The Industrial Revolution freed people from much physical labor, but it never truly freed us from repetitive cognitive work. AI is beginning to take on that work as well. In theory, people should have more time to think, create, care for others, and live.

But there is a problem: **optional work does not automatically create happy people.**

Work has a painful side. It brings pressure, exploitation, burnout, meaningless meetings, and task lists that never end.

But work has another side. It teaches responsibility, collaboration, judgment, and how to build something with other people. It also lets people feel that they still have some effect on the world. For many, work is not only a source of income. It is part of how they understand themselves.

If AI frees people from repetitive work and gives them more capacity to pursue what they truly value, that is good.

But if AI frees people from work only to let platforms feed them content, algorithms direct their attention, and a small group that owns the models and compute decide how they live, that is not happiness.

It looks more like Liu Cixin's *The Wages of Humanity* [7].

What makes *The Wages of Humanity* frightening is not only that humans are cared for. They are cared for until they lose upward mobility, their agency as a civilization, and their ability to redefine their own value. When a civilization can only wait for a superior power to distribute what people need to live, that is not a utopia. It is comfortable dependence.

The AI era carries a similar risk.

If a few companies control the strongest models, a few platforms control the distribution of attention, and a few countries control the policy thresholds while most people can only use whatever AI they are given, we may indeed no longer need to work.

But we may not need to think very much either.

## AI Has No Goal. People Must Steer.

I believe the most important skill in the AI era is knowing how to use data to steer AI.

AI has broader and deeper knowledge than any person, and it can act much faster. But AI has no goal. The direction and destination must come from people.

This sounds simple. But the further we move into 2026, the more important it feels.

When AI can write for you, do you still know what you want to say?

When AI can build a product for you, do you still know why that product should exist?

When AI can help run your company, do you still know where the company should go?

When AI can plan your life, do you still know whether the plan reflects what you truly want — or an average life inferred from everyone else's data?

The greatest danger may not be that AI replaces people.

It may be that AI convinces people that producing output is the same as thinking.

The flood of AI-generated content on Threads does not exhaust me because all of it is fake. It exhausts me because so much of it looks like thought but is really only a format. It has an opening, a list, a conclusion, and a quotable line. But no one takes real responsibility for it, and there is no lived experience behind it.

This is often where human value still lies.

Not in knowing more than AI, but in knowing why we care.

Not in writing faster than AI, but in accepting responsibility for a sentence.

Not in being more efficient than AI, but in still asking, "Is doing this actually better?"

## Three Measures of Happiness

So how should we judge whether AI is making people happier?

For now, I look at three things.

First, **has our thinking improved?**

If AI only helps us produce more content faster, our thinking has not necessarily improved. Better thinking means asking better questions. It means using AI to expose blind spots. It means turning an unclear intuition into a view that can be examined, discussed, and revised.

This does not happen as automatically as we might expect. Research has observed that the more confidence people place in AI, the less critical thinking they report doing. With AI, the task shifts from thinking for ourselves to checking whether AI is right [13]. More concerning, AI-generated ideas can raise the average quality of individual work while making different people's work more similar. Everyone clears the bar, but no one truly stands out [14].

Better thinking does not mean AI gives you more ideas. It means you understand your own ideas more clearly than you did without it. Using AI well does not help us avoid thinking. It makes avoidance harder.

Second, **has life improved?**

Where does the time saved by AI go?

One study followed knowledge workers using AI for six months. Participants spent about two fewer hours per week on email and did less work outside normal hours. But meetings did not decrease, and the overall composition of work did not meaningfully change [15]. Saved time does not automatically become life. It becomes room for another kind of work, or it is filled again with more digital content.

If the time AI saves is spent watching more short videos, consuming more AI-generated posts, or taking more courses that make us anxious, life has not improved. The anxiety of work has simply become the anxiety of self-improvement.

But if AI gives people more time with family, more time to care for their bodies, read, walk, eat with friends, or do things that no KPI measures but that make life feel worth living, then life has improved.

Third, **has control returned to people?**

This matters most to me.

It is also the hardest thing to measure. Research can measure productivity, psychological symptoms, and job satisfaction. It is much harder to measure whether people can still decide who they want to become. Yet that may be the central question of happiness.

If AI lets a few platforms understand us better while we understand ourselves less, that is not control.

If AI makes a company more dependent on external models while leaving it without data sovereignty or AI sovereignty, that is not control.

If AI policy lets a few closed-model companies weaken open competition in the name of safety, that is not control either.

Control does not mean everyone must train a model. It means people, companies, and societies must preserve the ability to choose, verify, migrate, audit, and refuse.

This is why I am building sovereign AI inside my company. It is also why I keep emphasizing TAE-AI: Transparent, Auditable, Explainable.

Transparency is not decoration.

Auditability is not paperwork.

Explainability is not about making AI appear well behaved.

They are how we keep people in the driver's seat.

## Are We Heading in the Right Direction?

I hope the answer is yes. But I cannot say that yet.

AI capabilities are advancing too fast. People are not keeping up. Institutions are not correcting course fast enough, and society is not sharing the gains fast enough. Models get stronger every day. Content becomes more abundant. AI courses become more anxiety-driven. Policy decisions become more concentrated among those already in power. Each development makes sense on its own. Together, they make me wonder: are we moving toward greater happiness, or simply racing toward greater efficiency?

Efficiency is not happiness. Output is not happiness. Stronger models are not happiness.

Happiness is closer to something else: knowing more clearly what you want and having more freedom to move toward it.

But there is a deeper issue that most AI discussions rarely touch.

Even if every AI policy is backed by strong evidence, one question remains: who gets to decide? What if only a small group of companies, think tanks, and regulators are in the room? If most affected people are excluded, then even a "correct" outcome does not make them more autonomous.

Happiness is not only about whether the outcome is good. It is also about who controls the process.

This is why I do not think the question has one right answer. AI can be all of these at once: a real productivity tool, a new knowledge monopoly, an accelerator of the attention economy, a weapon in national competition, and a chance for humanity to redefine its own value.

Model capability alone will not decide which force dominates. The people affected must still have a real say.

So, back to the title: has AI made us happier?

Not yet.

But I am not pessimistic. I simply do not believe happiness is a goal we can outsource to a model.

AI can help us reach a destination faster. It cannot tell us whether that destination is where we truly want to go. It can help us write more fluent sentences. It cannot decide which words are worth saying. It can help us manage our lives. It cannot answer what kind of life is worth living.

Those answers still require people.

People who keep asking questions, take responsibility for the answers, and decide the direction.

AI cannot do that for us.
